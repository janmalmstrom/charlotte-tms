TMS has FDA clearance for four specific indications. Here's what the clinical evidence shows — and what it doesn't.

Primary Indication — Most Common Use

Treatment-Resistant Depression

Treatment-resistant depression is defined as failing to respond adequately to at least two antidepressants at therapeutic doses. Roughly 30% of people with major depressive disorder meet this definition. TMS was first FDA-cleared for this indication in 2008.

🧠

Direct Neural Stimulation

TMS delivers focused magnetic pulses to the left DLPFC — the brain region consistently underactive in depression. It stimulates underactive circuits directly, bypassing the chemical approach of antidepressants entirely.

📊

~58% Response, ~37% Remission

The O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite trial and subsequent real-world effectiveness studies show approximately 58% of treatment-resistant patients respond. About 37% achieve full remission. Roughly 42% do not respond — we're honest about that.

✅

Insurance Covers It

BCBS, Cigna, Aetna, UHC, and Medicare all cover TMS for treatment-resistant depression — typically after 2 documented antidepressant failures with a DSM-5 MDD diagnosis. This is a major practical advantage over ketamine or ECT.

🚗

No Disruption to Daily Life

Each session is 20–40 minutes, outpatient, no sedation. Patients in Charlotte's banking and corporate sector routinely schedule sessions before work. No cognitive impairment, no driving restriction.

FDA-Cleared 2018

OCD (Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder)

TMS received FDA clearance for OCD in 2018 using the Brainsway deep TMS H7 coil, which targets deeper brain structures than standard TMS. It is indicated for adults whose OCD has not responded adequately to medication and behavioral therapy (ERP).

🎯

Deep TMS — Different Coil

OCD treatment uses the Brainsway H7 deep TMS coil, which targets the anterior cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal cortex — not the DLPFC used for depression. The protocol also involves brief symptom provocation before each session.

📉

Clinical Trial Results

The FDA-pivotal Carmi et al. trial showed a 38% response rate versus 11% placebo in treatment-resistant OCD patients — a statistically and clinically meaningful difference for a condition with very limited treatment options.

🔄

Adjunct to Existing Treatment

TMS for OCD is most effective when continued alongside medication management and ERP therapy. It is not a standalone replacement for behavioral treatment.

FDA-Cleared 2021

Anxious Depression

In 2021, the FDA cleared TMS specifically for major depressive disorder with anxious depression — a distinct clinical presentation combining significant depressive symptoms with high anxiety. This subtype responds poorly to standard antidepressants alone and is common in clinical practice.

🧩

A Common But Difficult Subtype

Anxious depression — depression with prominent anxiety symptoms — affects an estimated 40–50% of people with MDD. It is associated with slower medication response, higher relapse rates, and greater functional impairment.

⚡

Standard TMS Protocol

The same left DLPFC stimulation protocol used for standard depression is cleared for anxious depression. No change in number of sessions or equipment — the FDA clearance reflects outcome data specifically in this subgroup.

Emerging Protocol

Accelerated TMS / iTBS

Intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) is a newer TMS protocol that delivers the same clinical benefit in 3 minutes per session instead of 20–40. The Stanford SAINT protocol extends this to multiple sessions per day over 5 days — a full course in one week.

⏱️

3-Minute Sessions

iTBS delivers a burst-pattern stimulation that achieves equivalent neural activation in 3 minutes. The Blumberger et al. 2018 non-inferiority trial showed iTBS equals standard TMS in efficacy at one-tenth the session time.

📅

SAINT Protocol — 10 Days

The Stanford Accelerated Intelligent Neuromodulation Therapy (SAINT) protocol delivers 10 sessions per day for 5 days — 50 sessions total. Early trial data shows response rates above 80% in some treatment-resistant populations, though larger studies are ongoing.

💡

Who It's Best For

Patients who cannot commit to 6–8 weeks of daily treatment, or those with urgent need for faster response. Ask at your consultation whether accelerated protocols are available and appropriate for your case.

Cost

TMS Therapy Cost in Charlotte

TMS is substantially less expensive than many alternatives when insurance covers it. Here's a realistic picture.

🏥

With Insurance

With coverage from BCBS, Cigna, Aetna, or UHC, most patients pay standard specialist copays or coinsurance per session. After meeting your deductible, total out-of-pocket is typically $500–$3,000 for a full course.

💳

Without Insurance

A 36-session course costs approximately $7,000–$12,000 out of pocket. This is significantly less than ketamine therapy ($2,400–$3,600) for the initial series, but TMS runs longer. HSA and FSA funds are eligible.

🔄

Maintenance Sessions

After a successful initial course, some patients benefit from periodic maintenance sessions — typically once every 4–8 weeks. These are often covered by insurance at the same rate as treatment sessions.

📋

Prior Authorization

Most insurers require prior authorization for TMS. This requires documentation of your MDD diagnosis and antidepressant treatment history. We assist with prior authorization paperwork as part of the consultation process.

Insurance: Coverage depends on your specific plan and documented treatment history. We verify your benefits before any commitment. Medicare covers TMS for treatment-resistant depression; Medicaid coverage varies.
