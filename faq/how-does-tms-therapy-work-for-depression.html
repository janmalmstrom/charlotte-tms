How does TMS therapy work for depression?

Quick Answer: TMS therapy uses focused magnetic pulses to stimulate underactive neurons in the brain's left prefrontal cortex, producing antidepressant effects without medication, anesthesia, or systemic side effects.

Key Takeaways

  • TMS has been FDA-cleared for major depressive disorder since 2008, using 510(k) clearance — not a drug approval pathway.
  • The left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) is the primary treatment target; this region shows measurably reduced activity in patients with major depressive disorder.
  • In the landmark O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite randomized controlled trial, active TMS outperformed sham stimulation in treatment-resistant patients — approximately 58% of treatment-resistant patients show a meaningful clinical response across pooled data.
  • A standard TMS course is 36 sessions delivered over 6–8 weeks; most patients drive themselves to and from every session.
  • BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UnitedHealthcare, and Medicare typically cover TMS after documented failure of 2 or more antidepressants with a DSM-5 MDD diagnosis — though individual plan verification is required.

What TMS Does to Your Brain — The Mechanism in Plain Terms

TMS stands for transcranial magnetic stimulation. The device delivers brief, focused magnetic pulses through a coil held against your scalp. Those pulses generate a small electrical current in the cortical tissue directly beneath the coil — enough to depolarize neurons and trigger action potentials in targeted brain circuits.

The primary target for depression is the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). Neuroimaging studies have consistently shown that this region exhibits reduced metabolic activity in patients with major depressive disorder. By applying repetitive TMS (rTMS) at excitatory frequencies — typically 10 Hz for standard protocols — the treatment works to increase neuronal firing rates and, over a full course of therapy, to restore more typical patterns of activity in the prefrontal-limbic network that regulates mood, motivation, and executive function.

This is not a medication. Nothing enters your bloodstream. The mechanism is direct neural stimulation through an electromagnetic field, which is why TMS produces antidepressant effects without the systemic side effects associated with pharmacological agents.

💡 Clinical note: The left DLPFC target is localized using standardized anatomical methods or, in more precise protocols, neuronavigation based on the patient's own MRI. The coil placement is confirmed at the start of each session to ensure consistent targeting throughout the 6–8 week course.

The FDA Clearance History — Why 2008 Matters

TMS received its first 510(k) FDA clearance for major depressive disorder in 2008 — specifically the NeuroStar system from Neuronetics. This clearance was built substantially on the O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite randomized controlled trial published in Biological Psychiatry, which demonstrated statistically significant antidepressant effects of active rTMS over sham stimulation in patients who had not responded adequately to antidepressant medication.

This is FDA-cleared, not FDA-approved. TMS devices go through the 510(k) premarket notification pathway — the same pathway used for medical devices — rather than the new drug application (NDA) process used for pharmaceuticals. The distinction matters when discussing TMS with insurers and with patients comparing it to off-label treatments.

Since 2008, additional clearances have followed: OCD in 2018 (Brainsway H7 deep TMS system), anxious depression in 2021 (NeuroStar), and smoking cessation in 2020. According to NIMH brain stimulation research, TMS is among the most studied non-invasive neuromodulation techniques in psychiatry.

What the Response Rate Data Actually Shows

Across pooled clinical data, approximately 58% of treatment-resistant patients show a meaningful clinical response to TMS, and roughly 37% achieve full remission. Those numbers come from large observational datasets including the NeuroStar Advanced Therapy multicenter study, and they align with the direction of effect shown in the foundational O'Reardon et al. 2007 randomized controlled trial on PubMed.

It is equally important to be direct about what those numbers mean from the other direction: approximately 42% of treatment-resistant patients do not achieve an adequate response to TMS. TMS is not a guaranteed outcome. It is a clinically validated option with a meaningfully higher response rate than adding a third or fourth antidepressant to a regimen that has already failed twice — but it is not effective for everyone.

Who Is Most Likely to Respond to TMS?

  • Adults with a DSM-5 diagnosis of major depressive disorder who have failed 2 or more adequate antidepressant trials
  • Patients who have not failed a very large number of treatments (fewer prior failures generally correlates with better TMS response)
  • Patients without contraindications: no ferromagnetic metal implants in or near the head, no active seizure disorder
  • Patients able to commit to a full 36-session course — partial courses produce weaker outcomes
  • Patients who have had a prior response to TMS are strong candidates for retreatment or maintenance sessions

What a Standard TMS Course Looks Like — 36 Sessions Over 6–8 Weeks

A standard TMS course consists of 36 sessions delivered five days per week over approximately six to eight weeks. Each session runs 19–37 minutes depending on the protocol. You sit in a reclined chair, the treatment coil is positioned against your scalp, and the device delivers a series of magnetic pulses in timed intervals.

You are awake during every session. No anesthesia. No sedation. Most patients at our Charlotte TMS clinic drive themselves to and from every appointment — which matters practically for professionals working in Uptown Charlotte, South End, or along the I-77/I-85 corridor who need to return to work the same day.

Side Effects You Should Know About

TMS is not side-effect free. The most commonly reported side effects in clinical trials are headache (reported by approximately 30% of patients) and scalp discomfort at the coil site. These are typically mild and tend to decrease after the first one to two weeks of treatment as patients accommodate to the sensation. Serious adverse events — including seizure — are rare but documented, which is why every course of TMS at this clinic is conducted under board-certified psychiatrist oversight.

⚠️ Important: TMS is contraindicated in patients with ferromagnetic metal implants in or near the head (cochlear implants, aneurysm clips, certain deep brain stimulators). A safety screening questionnaire is completed before treatment begins. Tell your TMS provider about all implanted devices and any history of seizure disorder.

Insurance Coverage for TMS in Charlotte, NC

Most major insurers cover TMS for major depressive disorder after documented failure of at least two adequate antidepressant trials at therapeutic doses and duration. The insurers that typically provide coverage include BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UnitedHealthcare, and Medicare. Many Atrium Health and Novant Health employees in Mecklenburg County carry plans from these insurers through their employer benefits.

Coverage is not automatic. Your specific plan, your documented treatment history, and the prior authorization process all affect approval. The criteria most insurers require are a DSM-5 diagnosis of major depressive disorder, a minimum of two prior antidepressant failures at adequate dose and duration, and a prescribing psychiatrist's clinical documentation. You can verify your insurance coverage or request a consultation through our office — we complete prior authorizations as part of the intake process.

TMS is used alongside medication for many patients. Most people continue their existing psychiatric medications during and after the TMS course. Some patients reduce medication doses over time in collaboration with their psychiatrist — but this is an individualized clinical decision, not a standard outcome that can be promised at the outset.

Bottom Line

TMS therapy works by delivering focused magnetic pulses to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex to restore neuronal activity in a brain region that is measurably underactive in major depressive disorder. It is FDA-cleared (510(k)) since 2008, supported by randomized controlled trial data including O'Reardon et al. 2007, and covered by most major insurers — BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UHC, and Medicare — after documented antidepressant failure. Approximately 58% of treatment-resistant patients respond and 37% achieve remission; approximately 42% do not respond adequately. It is not a cure, and it is not right for every patient.
