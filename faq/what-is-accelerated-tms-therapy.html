What is accelerated TMS therapy?

Quick Answer: Accelerated TMS delivers multiple TMS sessions per day over 5 days rather than one session daily over 6–8 weeks, compressing the standard 36-session protocol into roughly a week.

Key Takeaways

  • Standard TMS therapy requires 36 sessions delivered once daily over 6–8 weeks; accelerated TMS delivers those sessions in 5 days using multiple daily pulses.
  • The Stanford SAINT protocol — studied by Cole et al. 2020 — used 10 sessions per day for 5 days (50 total sessions) and reported remission in 90% of severely treatment-resistant patients in a small controlled trial, though larger replication is needed.
  • Accelerated TMS typically uses intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS), a waveform shown to be non-inferior to standard rTMS in the Blumberger et al. 2018 Lancet trial.
  • Most major insurers — BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, and UnitedHealthcare — currently cover standard TMS but do not yet have consistent coverage policies for accelerated protocols; expect out-of-pocket costs to apply in most cases.
  • Accelerated TMS is best suited for patients who cannot complete a 6–8 week daily schedule, such as Charlotte professionals with demanding travel or work schedules, but it is not appropriate for everyone and requires careful clinical evaluation.

What Makes TMS "Accelerated" — and How Is It Different from Standard Treatment?

Standard TMS therapy for treatment-resistant depression follows a protocol of one session per day, five days per week, for approximately six to eight weeks — 36 sessions in total. That schedule was established in the pivotal O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite trial and remains the FDA-cleared benchmark for major depressive disorder.

Accelerated TMS compresses that same therapeutic dose into a much shorter window by delivering two, three, or even ten sessions within a single day. The most intensively studied version is the Stanford Accelerated Intelligent Neuromodulation Therapy protocol — better known as SAINT — developed at Stanford University and evaluated by Cole et al. in a 2020 trial published in PubMed (Cole et al. 2020, SAINT accelerated TMS trial). That trial delivered 10 sessions daily for 5 consecutive days — 50 sessions total — and reported remission in 90% of a small cohort of severely treatment-resistant patients. Those results were striking. They were also generated in a tightly controlled research setting with a sample size that requires broader replication before that remission figure can be applied to general clinical practice.

The Science Behind the Compressed Schedule: iTBS

Most accelerated protocols use intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) rather than the repetitive TMS (rTMS) pulses used in the original FDA-cleared 10 Hz protocol. iTBS delivers bursts of high-frequency magnetic pulses in a pattern that mimics natural theta rhythms in the brain, stimulating the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) — the region consistently underactive in major depressive disorder — in approximately 3 minutes rather than the 37-minute sessions typical of standard rTMS.

The clinical validation for iTBS as a standalone approach comes from the Blumberger et al. 2018 non-inferiority trial published in The Lancet (PubMed — Blumberger et al. 2018), which enrolled 414 patients across multiple Canadian centers and found iTBS produced equivalent antidepressant effects to standard 10 Hz rTMS. That non-inferiority finding is what makes compressed daily scheduling feasible — each iTBS session is short enough to stack multiple treatments within a single clinical day without excessive patient burden.

💡 Clinical note: iTBS sessions typically last 3–4 minutes each. In a protocol delivering 3 sessions per day with inter-session intervals of at least 15–50 minutes, a patient's total daily clinic time may still be under 2 hours — relevant for Charlotte professionals managing demanding schedules at employers like Bank of America, Truist, or Atrium Health who cannot commit to six to eight weeks of daily morning appointments.

Who Is Accelerated TMS Designed For?

Accelerated TMS is not a universal upgrade over standard TMS. It is most clinically relevant for specific patient situations:

  • Scheduling constraints: Patients who cannot commit to a 6–8 week daily schedule — traveling executives, healthcare workers with rotating shifts at Novant Health or Atrium Health, or professionals whose roles require significant out-of-state travel along Charlotte's I-77/I-85 business corridor.
  • Urgent symptom severity: Patients with severe treatment-resistant depression who need a faster path to potential relief and have already failed multiple antidepressant trials.
  • Research or trial participants: Some Charlotte-area patients may be eligible for accelerated TMS through academic or clinical research programs affiliated with institutions such as UNC Charlotte or regional health systems.

Accelerated TMS is not appropriate for patients with poorly controlled seizure disorders, certain cardiac devices, or metal implants near the head. A full clinical screening — including psychiatric evaluation and a review of prior medication trials — is required before any TMS protocol begins, accelerated or standard.

Insurance Coverage: Where Accelerated TMS Stands Right Now

This is where the honest clinical picture diverges significantly from the research excitement. Standard TMS — 36 sessions, once daily — is covered by most major insurers operating in North Carolina, including BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UnitedHealthcare, and Medicare, when a patient has a DSM-5 major depressive disorder diagnosis and has documented failure of at least two adequate antidepressant trials.

Accelerated TMS protocols, including SAINT, do not currently have consistent commercial payer coverage policies in North Carolina. Because the clinical evidence base — while promising — is still developing, most insurers classify accelerated protocols as investigational for standard billing purposes. Patients pursuing accelerated TMS should anticipate out-of-pocket costs, which vary by clinic and protocol structure.

⚠️ Important: If a provider tells you accelerated TMS is "definitely covered" by your BCBS NC or Cigna plan, verify that claim directly with your insurer before beginning treatment. Coverage policies for accelerated protocols are evolving and plan-specific. The licensed Charlotte TMS providers we connect you with can help you navigate that verification process — but the insurer's written determination is the authoritative answer.

Side Effects and Honest Expectations

Accelerated TMS carries the same side effect profile as standard TMS, potentially intensified by the compressed schedule. Headache and scalp discomfort are the most commonly reported side effects across TMS trials, and some patients undergoing accelerated protocols report these effects more acutely in the first two to three days due to higher daily stimulation volume. These effects are generally mild and tend to diminish as the course progresses. Serious adverse events, including seizure, are rare but are part of the informed consent discussion every patient should have before treatment begins.

On outcomes: the 90% remission figure from Cole et al. 2020 is the most-cited accelerated TMS result, but it comes from a small, highly selected sample in a controlled research setting. Standard TMS for treatment-resistant depression produces approximately a 58% response rate and 37% remission rate in larger real-world datasets. Accelerated protocols may perform comparably or better in certain patients — however, approximately 42% of treatment-resistant patients do not achieve a meaningful response regardless of TMS delivery schedule. Honest pre-treatment counseling on realistic expectations is a clinical obligation, not a footnote.

Accelerated TMS: Key Clinical Facts

  • Compresses 36+ sessions into 5 days using multiple daily iTBS treatments
  • iTBS validated as non-inferior to standard rTMS in Blumberger et al. 2018 (The Lancet, n=414)
  • SAINT protocol (Cole et al. 2020) reported 90% remission in a small controlled trial — larger replication is ongoing
  • TMS is FDA-cleared, not FDA-approved; accelerated protocols are not independently FDA-cleared and are offered under the broader iTBS clearance
  • Not consistently covered by BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, or UHC as of 2025 — standard TMS remains the insured option for most patients
  • Side effects mirror standard TMS: headache and scalp discomfort are most common and may be more pronounced in the first few days of an accelerated course
  • Requires full psychiatric evaluation and documented antidepressant failure before any protocol begins
Bottom Line

Accelerated TMS — particularly the SAINT iTBS protocol — represents a clinically meaningful development for patients who cannot complete a standard 6–8 week TMS schedule or who need a faster treatment course due to severity. The early trial data is genuinely encouraging. This is not a standard-of-care protocol yet: insurance coverage in North Carolina is inconsistent, the evidence base is still maturing, and roughly 42% of treatment-resistant patients do not respond to TMS regardless of delivery schedule.
