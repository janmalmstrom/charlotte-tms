What does a TMS session feel like?

Quick Answer: A TMS session feels like a series of rhythmic tapping or knocking sensations on your scalp, lasting 20–40 minutes, with no sedation required — you drive yourself home afterward.

Key Takeaways

  • Each TMS session lasts approximately 20–40 minutes depending on the protocol; you remain awake and alert throughout and can return to work immediately after.
  • The sensation is most commonly described as a rhythmic tapping or woodpecker-like knocking on the scalp — not painful for most patients, though some experience mild discomfort during the first few sessions.
  • Headache and scalp discomfort are the most commonly reported side effects, affecting a significant minority of patients in clinical trials, including the O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite trial — these typically diminish after the first week.
  • A standard course of TMS for treatment-resistant depression is 36 sessions delivered over 6–8 weeks; the full therapeutic effect generally builds gradually across that period.
  • Most major insurers — including BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, and UnitedHealthcare — cover TMS after two documented antidepressant failures with a DSM-5 MDD diagnosis; you can verify your specific coverage before beginning.

What Actually Happens During a TMS Session

When you arrive for a TMS session at our Charlotte TMS clinic, you sit in a reclined chair — fully clothed, fully awake. There is no anesthesia, no IV line, and no recovery room. A technician positions a magnetic coil against the left side of your scalp, over a region of the brain called the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). This is the area most consistently found to be underactive in major depressive disorder.

Once the coil is in place, the device delivers brief, focused magnetic pulses. Each pulse generates a small electrical current in the targeted neurons. During active stimulation, you hear a rapid clicking sound and feel a tapping or knocking sensation on your scalp — often described as rhythmic, mechanical, and unusual rather than sharp or painful. Between pulse trains, the coil pauses. You are awake for all of it.

A standard session using a conventional repetitive TMS (rTMS) protocol takes approximately 37–40 minutes. Newer intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) protocols — studied in Blumberger et al. 2018 in The Lancet — deliver an equivalent therapeutic dose in approximately 3 minutes per session, and this protocol is now commonly used at accredited clinics. When your session ends, you stand up, collect your belongings, and drive yourself home or back to the office.

Does TMS Hurt? What the First Session Is Like

Pain tolerance varies, and it is worth setting accurate expectations. Most patients find TMS uncomfortable rather than painful, particularly during the first several sessions. The sensation can feel more intense at the scalp surface at the start of treatment, before the surrounding tissues adapt to the stimulation.

The most commonly reported side effects in clinical trials — including the O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite pivotal trial and subsequent post-market data — are headache and scalp discomfort. These are real and occur in a meaningful minority of patients. They are not trivial to dismiss, but they are also generally manageable: most patients find that discomfort peaks in the first week and diminishes significantly by week two. Over-the-counter analgesics taken before a session can help.

⚠️ Important: TMS does not have a "no side effects" profile. Headaches and scalp discomfort occur in a significant minority of patients and are the side effects most frequently reported in clinical trials. Rarely, patients experience facial muscle twitching during stimulation. Seizure is a very rare but documented risk, estimated at less than 1 in 10,000 treatment courses, and is why a physician screens every patient before treatment begins.

What a Full Course of TMS Looks Like — Session by Session

A standard course of TMS for treatment-resistant depression is 36 sessions, delivered five days a week over approximately 6–8 weeks. For many Charlotte patients who work in finance at Bank of America, Truist, or Wells Fargo's regional offices — or in healthcare at Atrium Health or Novant Health — the outpatient format is what makes this feasible. Sessions are typically scheduled in the morning before work, at lunch, or at the end of the business day.

  1. Sessions 1–5 (Week 1): Your treatment team confirms the motor threshold — the individualized intensity setting calibrated to your neurobiology. Expect the most scalp discomfort this week. Most patients notice little mood change yet.
  2. Sessions 6–15 (Weeks 2–3): The stimulation becomes more familiar. Some patients begin to notice subtle changes — sleep quality, energy, or irritability shifting before mood itself improves.
  3. Sessions 16–30 (Weeks 4–6): This is the window in which most responders begin to notice meaningful improvement in depressive symptoms. The George et al. 2010 trial, published in Archives of General Psychiatry, documented response building progressively across this period.
  4. Sessions 31–36 (Weeks 7–8): Consolidation phase. Patients who responded continue to see gains; final clinical assessment is scheduled near the end of this phase.
💡 Clinical note: TMS does not produce an immediate mood lift the way some medications or other interventions might. The therapeutic effect is cumulative — the brain is being retrained to sustain activation in circuits that have been underactive. Patients who expect dramatic change in week one are often disappointed; patients who reach week four frequently report genuine improvement. According to APA patient resources on TMS therapy, the full clinical response typically emerges over the complete course of treatment.

What You Can Do During a Session

Because you are fully awake and alert, you are not required to do anything in particular. Many patients listen to music or a podcast through earbuds (with volume awareness — the clicking is loud). Some read. Some simply sit. You cannot use your phone to scroll heavily during active stimulation because of the clicking noise and the need to keep your head still, but the requirement to remain motionless is minimal — normal head position, not rigid stillness.

You will be asked not to cross your legs or fold your hands during the session, to avoid accidentally altering the electrical field. Beyond that, there is no preparation ritual, no fasting requirement, and no recovery time. Many patients in the Mecklenburg County area commute from Matthews, Huntersville, or across the I-77/I-85 corridor and return directly to their day.

TMS Session — What to Expect at a Glance

  • Duration: 3–40 minutes per session depending on protocol (iTBS vs. conventional rTMS)
  • Sensation: rhythmic tapping or knocking on the scalp; unusual, not typically painful
  • Most common side effects: headache and scalp discomfort, most prominent in week one
  • Total course: 36 sessions over 6–8 weeks, five days per week
  • No sedation, no anesthesia — you drive yourself to and from every session
  • Response rate in treatment-resistant depression: ~58%; approximately 42% of patients do not respond
  • Coverage: BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UHC, Medicare — after 2+ antidepressant failures; prior authorization required
Bottom Line

A TMS session is an outpatient procedure lasting 3–40 minutes in which you sit awake while a magnetic coil delivers rhythmic pulses to the left DLPFC. Most patients find the sensation manageable after the first week, though headaches and scalp discomfort are genuine and documented side effects — not a reason to avoid treatment, but an honest part of the picture. The full 36-session course produces a meaningful response in approximately 58% of treatment-resistant patients; roughly 42% do not respond. TMS is not a cure, and it does not work for everyone. But for patients who have tried two or more antidepressants without adequate relief, it is an FDA-cleared, outpatient, insurance-covered option worth evaluating with a physician.
