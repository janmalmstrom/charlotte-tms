What is the success rate of TMS for depression?

Quick Answer: In clinical trials of treatment-resistant depression, TMS produces a meaningful response in approximately 58% of patients, with around 37% achieving full remission.

Key Takeaways

  • The landmark O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite trial established TMS as effective for treatment-resistant depression; subsequent real-world data (NeuroStar TMS Therapy Patient Registry) confirmed ~58% response and ~37% remission rates.
  • Roughly 42% of patients do not respond to TMS — honest expectations matter, and a thorough psychiatric evaluation helps identify who is most likely to benefit.
  • A standard TMS course is 36 sessions over 6–8 weeks, delivered as outpatient visits; no anesthesia, no sedation, and you drive yourself to and from every appointment.
  • BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UnitedHealthcare, and Medicare typically cover TMS after two documented antidepressant failures with a DSM-5 major depressive disorder diagnosis.
  • Accelerated protocols such as the Stanford SAINT iTBS design (Cole et al. 2020) compress treatment into five days; these are increasingly available but represent a different protocol than the standard 36-session course.

What the Clinical Data Actually Show

If you've tried two or more antidepressants without adequate relief, you have treatment-resistant depression — a condition affecting roughly 30% of people diagnosed with major depressive disorder, according to NIMH brain stimulation research. TMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation) has been FDA-cleared for treatment-resistant depression since 2008, and its success rates come from a body of controlled research, not marketing copy.

The most frequently cited figures come from two sources. First, the O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite randomized controlled trial — the pivotal study supporting FDA clearance — demonstrated statistically significant improvement in active TMS versus sham stimulation in patients who had failed prior antidepressant trials. Second, the NeuroStar TMS Therapy Patient Registry, a large naturalistic study of patients treated in real clinical settings, reported approximately 58% of patients achieving a meaningful reduction in depression symptoms (response), with approximately 37% reaching full remission. George et al. 2010, an independently conducted sham-controlled trial, corroborated these findings in patients who had failed at least one adequate antidepressant trial.

These numbers reflect treatment-resistant patients — people who had already not responded to medication. That context matters when you're interpreting what "58% response" means for you personally.

How TMS Works on the Depressed Brain

TMS delivers focused magnetic pulses to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) — a region consistently found to be underactive in major depressive disorder. Each pulse induces a small electrical current that stimulates neurons in that area. Over a full course of 36 sessions, this repeated stimulation appears to strengthen neural circuits involved in mood regulation, gradually normalizing activity in networks that connect the prefrontal cortex to deeper limbic structures.

Unlike antidepressants, TMS acts directly on a targeted brain region rather than altering systemic neurotransmitter levels throughout your body. This is why patients who have not responded to SSRIs, SNRIs, or augmentation strategies may still respond to TMS — the mechanism is different, not simply a stronger version of the same intervention.

💡 Clinical note: TMS and medication are not mutually exclusive. Most patients continue their current psychiatric medications throughout a TMS course. For some patients, depression improves enough over time that a prescribing psychiatrist may later consider dose adjustments — but TMS is not a substitute for medication management, and no outcome regarding medication reduction can be promised in advance.

Who Responds Best — and Who May Not

Response to TMS is not uniform. Research and clinical experience suggest several factors associated with better outcomes:

  • Fewer prior antidepressant failures (one or two, rather than five or six)
  • Shorter duration of the current depressive episode
  • Absence of active psychotic features
  • A diagnosis of unipolar major depressive disorder (rather than bipolar depression, though TMS is used off-label in bipolar depression at some centers)
  • Completing the full 36-session protocol without interruption

Approximately 42% of patients do not achieve a meaningful response. If you fall into that group, it does not mean further options are exhausted — your psychiatrist may consider augmentation strategies, protocol adjustments, or referral for evaluation of other brain stimulation modalities. If you are experiencing severe depression with thoughts of self-harm, please contact the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline by calling or texting 988 before waiting for any outpatient treatment course to begin.

⚠️ Important: TMS is a treatment, not a cure. Response and remission rates are population-level statistics — your individual outcome depends on your specific diagnosis, depression history, and treatment history. A psychiatric evaluation at our clinic will give you a more individualized picture before you commit to a course of treatment.

Accelerated TMS: Does Faster Mean Better Results?

Standard TMS runs 36 sessions over six to eight weeks. Accelerated protocols — most prominently the Stanford SAINT (Standardized, Neuronavigated, Accelerated, Intelligent TMS) design studied by Cole et al. 2020 in The American Journal of Psychiatry — condense treatment into approximately 10 sessions over five consecutive days using intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS). The Cole et al. 2020 study reported remission rates exceeding 78% in a small cohort of highly treatment-resistant patients, a striking finding that has driven significant interest.

Blumberger et al. 2018 in The Lancet established that iTBS (the pulse pattern used in accelerated protocols) was non-inferior to standard 10 Hz TMS for efficacy, at roughly one-third the session time. Accelerated TMS represents a genuine option for some patients, particularly those who cannot commit to six to eight weeks of daily appointments — a real consideration for professionals working in Charlotte's banking corridor or in clinical roles at Atrium Health or Novant Health. That said, accelerated protocols are not yet as extensively validated as the standard 36-session course, and not all insurers cover them under the same criteria.

Insurance Coverage for TMS in Charlotte and Mecklenburg County

For most Charlotte-area patients, cost is not a barrier if you meet clinical criteria. The following major insurers typically cover a standard TMS course for treatment-resistant major depressive disorder:

  • Blue Cross Blue Shield of North Carolina (BCBS NC)
  • Cigna
  • Aetna
  • UnitedHealthcare (UHC)
  • Medicare (under the Medicare Local Coverage Determination for TMS)

Standard criteria across these plans typically require: a DSM-5 diagnosis of major depressive disorder, documented failure of at least two adequate antidepressant trials at therapeutic doses and duration, and a psychiatrist referral or supervising prescriber. TMS Success Rate: Key Clinical Facts

  • ~58% response rate and ~37% remission rate in treatment-resistant depression (NeuroStar Patient Registry; George et al. 2010)
  • ~42% of patients do not respond — honest data, not a disqualifier for trying
  • Standard protocol: 36 outpatient sessions over 6–8 weeks; no anesthesia or sedation required
  • Most common side effects: headache and scalp discomfort, typically mild and transient
  • FDA-cleared since 2008 for major depressive disorder (510(k) clearance)
  • Covered by BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UHC, and Medicare after 2+ antidepressant failures
  • Accelerated iTBS protocols (Cole et al. 2020) show promise but are a different — and less extensively validated — protocol than the standard course

A Note on Side Effects

TMS does not carry the systemic side effect profile of antidepressant medications — no weight gain, sexual dysfunction, or gastrointestinal effects. However, it is not side-effect-free. Headache and scalp discomfort at the treatment site are the most commonly reported effects, occurring in a significant minority of patients across clinical trials. These effects are typically mild, occur during or shortly after sessions, and tend to diminish over the first week of treatment. Serious adverse events — including seizure — are rare but documented; your treating physician will review your full history to identify any contraindications before starting.

Bottom Line

TMS produces a meaningful response in approximately 58% of treatment-resistant depression patients and full remission in approximately 37%, based on the NeuroStar Patient Registry and corroborated by George et al. 2010. Roughly 42% of patients do not respond — a fact worth knowing before you start. TMS is FDA-cleared (510(k) clearance, 2008), covered by most major insurers after two antidepressant failures, and delivered as an outpatient procedure requiring no anesthesia.
