How TMS Works
How does TMS therapy work?

TMS delivers focused magnetic pulses to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) — the brain region consistently underactive in depression. The magnetic field induces a small electrical current in neurons, stimulating activity in circuits that haven't been responding normally. Unlike antidepressants, no drug enters your system. Sessions run 20–40 minutes and you are fully awake throughout.

How is TMS different from antidepressants?

Antidepressants — SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs — adjust neurotransmitter levels chemically throughout your entire system. TMS works physically, using the same magnetic field physics as an MRI to stimulate a specific brain region directly. No drug enters your body. No systemic side effects. That's why it can work when medications haven't — it bypasses the chemical pathway entirely and targets the neural circuit directly.

Is TMS FDA approved?

TMS uses FDA 510(k) clearance — the regulatory pathway for medical devices — rather than the drug NDA process. It is FDA-cleared for: treatment-resistant depression (2008, NeuroStar first cleared), OCD (2018, Brainsway deep TMS), anxious depression (2021), and smoking cessation (2020). "FDA-cleared" is the accurate term; both pathways represent FDA authorization to market for the specific indication.

What is the DLPFC and why does TMS target it?

The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) is a region of the frontal lobe involved in mood regulation, executive function, and cognitive control. Neuroimaging studies consistently show reduced activity in the left DLPFC in patients with major depression. TMS targets this region with repetitive magnetic pulses to increase its activity — directly addressing the neural dysfunction associated with depression rather than adjusting neurotransmitter chemistry.

What is iTBS and how is it different from standard TMS?

Intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) is a TMS protocol that delivers burst-pattern magnetic pulses mimicking natural brain rhythms. The Blumberger et al. 2018 non-inferiority trial (published in The Lancet) showed iTBS is equally effective as standard rTMS but takes only 3 minutes per session instead of 20–40. It has the same FDA clearance for treatment-resistant depression and is now the standard at many clinics due to the time advantage.

Treatment-Resistant Depression
What is the success rate of TMS for depression?

Published clinical data shows approximately 58% of treatment-resistant patients respond to TMS — meaning a clinically meaningful reduction in depression symptoms. About 37% achieve full remission. Roughly 42% do not respond. These figures come from the O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite randomized controlled trial and subsequent real-world effectiveness studies. We are transparent about the non-response rate because patients deserve accurate expectations.

How many antidepressants do I need to have failed before TMS?

Most insurance plans require documented failure of at least 2 antidepressants at therapeutic doses for an adequate duration (typically 6–8 weeks each) before approving TMS coverage. Some plans require 4. The clinical definition of treatment-resistant depression used by researchers is 2+ failures, which also happens to match the most common insurance prior authorization threshold.

Can TMS help if I've failed ECT?

ECT and TMS work through different mechanisms — ECT produces a generalized seizure affecting the entire brain; TMS targets a specific cortical region without triggering a seizure. Some patients who have not responded to ECT respond to TMS, but the evidence base for TMS post-ECT is limited. A physician consultation is necessary to evaluate whether TMS is appropriate after ECT failure.

Should I stop my antidepressants before TMS?

Generally no. Most patients continue their existing medications during a TMS course. Some combinations require adjustment — particularly bupropion (Wellbutrin), which lowers the seizure threshold, and medications that significantly affect cortical excitability. Your physician will review your full medication list before the first session. Never stop psychiatric medications without medical guidance.

OCD
Does TMS work for OCD?

Yes — TMS received FDA clearance for OCD in 2018 using the Brainsway deep TMS H7 coil. The pivotal Carmi et al. trial showed a 38% response rate versus 11% for sham — a clinically meaningful difference for a condition with limited treatment options. It targets deeper brain structures (anterior cingulate cortex, medial prefrontal cortex) than the DLPFC targeted for depression. The protocol also involves brief symptom provocation before each session to enhance the neural targeting.

Is the TMS protocol for OCD different from depression?

Yes, in two ways. First, OCD treatment requires the Brainsway H7 deep TMS coil — not the standard figure-8 coil used for depression. Second, each session includes a brief "provocation" step: the patient is exposed to OCD-triggering stimuli for 30 seconds immediately before TMS. This activates the OCD circuit, making it more responsive to the subsequent magnetic stimulation. Not all TMS clinics have the Brainsway system — confirm equipment availability when choosing a provider.

The TMS Session
What does a TMS session feel like?

You sit in a reclined chair with a magnetic coil positioned against your scalp over the left DLPFC. During the session you feel a tapping or clicking sensation on your scalp in rhythm with the magnetic pulses — like a woodpecker tapping. Some patients find this mildly uncomfortable at first; most adapt within a few sessions. You remain fully awake and can talk, listen to music, or watch a show. Sessions run 20–40 minutes for standard rTMS, or 3 minutes for iTBS.

Can I drive after TMS?

Yes. TMS involves no sedation and causes no cognitive impairment. You are fully alert immediately after each session. Most Charlotte patients drive themselves to and from every session and return directly to work. This is a meaningful practical advantage over ketamine infusions, which require a driver for every appointment.

How long does a full TMS course take?

Standard protocol: 36 sessions delivered 5 times per week for approximately 7–8 weeks. The time commitment is the most common challenge patients raise — 36 morning appointments over 8 weeks is a real logistical ask. Accelerated iTBS addresses this by reducing each session to 3 minutes. The Stanford SAINT accelerated protocol compresses an entire course into 5 days (10 sessions/day), though this is not yet widely available.

When do patients start feeling better with TMS?

Most patients who respond to TMS begin noticing improvement between weeks 3 and 5 of treatment — typically after 15–25 sessions. Some notice subtle changes earlier; a minority notice improvement only after completing the full course. Unlike ketamine, TMS does not produce rapid onset effects within hours or days. If no response is seen after 4–5 weeks, a physician will reassess the treatment plan.

Safety & Side Effects
What are the side effects of TMS?

Common: Scalp discomfort or tapping sensation at the treatment site during sessions. Mild headache after treatment — usually resolves within an hour, responsive to OTC pain relievers. Both improve with each session as patients adapt. Rare: Seizure risk is less than 0.1% — this is why patients with seizure history or metal implants near the head are excluded. Not present: Weight changes, sexual side effects, gastrointestinal effects, memory impairment, cognitive dulling. TMS has no systemic side effects because no drug is used.

Who cannot have TMS?

Hard contraindications: metal implants in or near the head — cochlear implants, deep brain stimulators, aneurysm clips, metal plates in the skull. These can be dislodged or heated by the magnetic field. Also contraindicated: active seizure disorder or history of epilepsy (raises seizure risk). Relative contraindications requiring careful evaluation: cardiac pacemakers, history of head injury, pregnancy. Dental fillings, braces, and joint replacements outside the head are not contraindications.

Does TMS cause memory loss?

No. Memory impairment is associated with ECT (electroconvulsive therapy), not TMS. TMS does not induce a seizure and does not cause memory loss. This is one of the primary clinical advantages of TMS over ECT for treatment-resistant depression — equal or better efficacy data in many comparisons, without the cognitive side effects that cause many patients to decline ECT.

Cost & Insurance
Does insurance cover TMS in Charlotte?

Yes — most major commercial insurers cover TMS for treatment-resistant depression in North Carolina. BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, and UnitedHealthcare all have TMS coverage policies. Standard prior authorization requirements: DSM-5 MDD diagnosis, documented failure of at least 2 antidepressants at adequate doses, and in some cases failure of augmentation therapy. Medicare covers TMS. Medicaid coverage varies. We verify your specific plan benefits before any treatment begins.

How much does TMS cost out of pocket in Charlotte?

Without insurance: a full 36-session course is approximately $7,000–$12,000. With insurance coverage, most patients pay specialist copays or coinsurance — total out of pocket after deductible is typically $500–$3,000. HSA and FSA funds are eligible for TMS. If you have coverage through a Charlotte employer — Bank of America, Truist, Atrium Health, or a similar large employer — benefits are often strong. We provide a detailed cost estimate after verifying your specific plan.

Does Medicare cover TMS for depression?

Yes. Medicare covers TMS for treatment-resistant major depressive disorder as of 2020, under a national coverage determination (NCD). Coverage requires documentation of MDD and adequate prior antidepressant treatment. Medicare Advantage plans generally follow original Medicare coverage policies for TMS, though specific plan details vary. Contact your plan to confirm your specific benefits.

Choosing a TMS Clinic
What should I look for in a TMS clinic in Charlotte?

Key criteria: (1) Psychiatrist oversight — a board-certified psychiatrist should evaluate every patient and supervise the treatment course. (2) FDA-cleared equipment — NeuroStar, Brainsway, MagVenture, or Magstim systems cleared by the FDA. (3) Insurance verification upfront — a reputable clinic confirms your coverage before you commit. (4) Honest candidacy assessment — if TMS is unlikely to help you, they should say so. (5) Coordination with your existing psychiatrist — TMS is most effective when integrated with ongoing psychiatric care.

Can my psychiatrist refer me to TMS?

Yes — and a referral from your treating psychiatrist can significantly help with insurance prior authorization, since it documents your treatment history and the medical necessity of TMS. Some insurers require a referral; others allow self-referral. Either way, keeping your existing psychiatrist informed and involved in your TMS course improves outcomes and continuity of care.

TMS vs Other Treatments
TMS vs ketamine — which is better for depression?

They target different mechanisms and suit different situations. TMS: FDA-cleared, insurance-covered, no dissociation, outpatient with no driving restriction, slower onset (weeks), ~58% response rate. Ketamine: off-label for depression, cash-pay (~$2,400–$3,600 for 6 infusions), produces results within 24 hours, ~70% response rate in treatment-resistant cases, requires a driver. Ketamine's speed advantage matters for acute crisis; TMS's insurance coverage and no-sedation profile suit patients managing ongoing treatment. Many patients try TMS first given insurance coverage, then consider ketamine if TMS doesn't work.

TMS vs ECT — which is less invasive?

TMS is significantly less invasive. ECT requires general anesthesia, induces a controlled seizure, and is associated with memory impairment — particularly autobiographical memory loss. TMS requires no anesthesia, causes no seizure (at therapeutic settings), and has no cognitive side effects. ECT has a higher remission rate in severely treatment-resistant or psychotic depression; TMS is appropriate for patients who are not ECT candidates or who prefer to avoid ECT's risks.

TMS vs antidepressants — which works better for treatment-resistant depression?

For patients who have already failed 2+ antidepressants, adding another antidepressant produces a response in roughly 10–30% of cases. TMS produces a response in approximately 58% of the same population — a substantially better outcome. This is why insurance covers TMS after antidepressant failure: the clinical evidence supports it as the next appropriate step. TMS does not replace antidepressants; most patients continue existing medications during and after TMS.
