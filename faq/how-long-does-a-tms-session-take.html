How long does a TMS session take?

Quick Answer: A standard TMS session for depression takes 19–37 minutes depending on the protocol used; most patients complete 36 sessions over 6–8 weeks.

Key Takeaways

  • Standard NeuroStar TMS sessions run approximately 19–37 minutes; newer intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) protocols can be completed in as little as 3 minutes per session.
  • The full TMS course for treatment-resistant depression is 36 sessions administered 5 days per week over 6–8 weeks — not a one-time procedure.
  • Accelerated protocols such as Stanford SAINT compress 10 sessions into 5 days, as studied by Cole et al. 2020, offering a faster timeline for eligible patients.
  • Most Bank of America, Atrium Health, and Novant Health employee plans administered through BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, or UHC cover TMS after 2+ documented antidepressant failures — but session-count requirements are part of what insurers audit, so completing the full 36-session course matters for coverage.
  • Roughly 58% of treatment-resistant patients show a meaningful response to TMS; approximately 42% do not — honest expectations from the start help you plan your schedule and commit to the full protocol.

The Clinical Answer: Session Length Depends on the Protocol

A single TMS session for major depressive disorder runs between 19 and 37 minutes under standard repetitive TMS (rTMS) protocols. The exact duration depends on which FDA-cleared device and stimulation pattern your provider uses. NeuroStar, one of the most widely deployed systems, delivers a standard session in approximately 19 minutes. Older figure-of-eight coil protocols using 10 Hz stimulation can run closer to 37 minutes per visit.

The overall course is consistent across protocols: 36 sessions, delivered five days per week, over 6–8 weeks. That structure is not arbitrary — the O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite trial that formed the clinical basis for FDA clearance of TMS in 2008 used this 6-week framework, and subsequent real-world data have supported it. Skipping sessions or stopping early compromises outcomes.

If you work in Charlotte's banking district or at Atrium Health or Novant Health and are calculating whether TMS fits around a full-time schedule: most patients manage it. A 19–37 minute appointment, five mornings or lunches per week for six weeks, is the realistic commitment.

iTBS: The 3-Minute Session Option

Intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) is an FDA-cleared alternative to standard rTMS that compresses the magnetic pulse pattern into approximately 3 minutes per session. Blumberger et al. published the pivotal non-inferiority trial in The Lancet in 2018 (the THREE-D trial), demonstrating that iTBS produces equivalent clinical outcomes to standard 37-minute rTMS in treatment-resistant depression — in a fraction of the time.

Charlotte TMS providers using iTBS-capable systems can offer significantly shorter in-office visits. The 36-session, 6-week course structure generally remains the same; what changes is chair time per day. For patients commuting the I-77 or I-85 corridor to uptown Charlotte, iTBS can make adherence substantially more realistic.

💡 Clinical note: Ask specifically whether the Charlotte TMS provider you're connecting with uses standard 10 Hz rTMS or an iTBS protocol. Both are FDA-cleared. The session duration difference — up to 37 minutes versus roughly 3 minutes — is significant for scheduling purposes, even though clinical outcomes appear equivalent per Blumberger et al. 2018.

Accelerated TMS: 10 Sessions in 5 Days

For patients who cannot commit to a 6-week outpatient schedule, or who need faster symptom relief, accelerated TMS protocols are worth discussing with your provider. The Stanford SAINT (Stanford Accelerated Intelligent Neuromodulation Therapy) protocol, described by Cole et al. in 2020, delivers 10 iTBS sessions per day over 5 consecutive days — compressing what would otherwise be two weeks of treatment into a single work week.

Early data on SAINT showed remission rates higher than standard TMS in a small sample of patients with severe treatment-resistant depression; larger randomized trials are ongoing. This protocol is not appropriate for every patient, and not all Charlotte-area providers offer it. If accelerated TMS is a priority, confirm availability and eligibility criteria before scheduling a consultation.

What the Full 6–8 Weeks Looks Like Week by Week

  1. Weeks 1–2: A motor threshold mapping session adds approximately 10 minutes to day one; daily sessions then begin. Some patients experience mild scalp discomfort or headache in the first week as they adjust to stimulation.
  2. Weeks 3–4: Stimulation continues at the same parameters. Among patients who ultimately respond, early indicators — improved sleep, modestly increased motivation — may begin to appear during this window. Absence of response by week 4 does not rule out eventual benefit.
  3. Weeks 5–6: Core treatment weeks. Symptom scales (PHQ-9, MADRS) are typically administered to track response.
  4. Weeks 7–8 (if prescribed): Some protocols extend to 36 or more sessions for patients with partial response. The treating clinician determines whether additional sessions are warranted based on your symptom trajectory.
⚠️ Important: TMS is not a single appointment. Insurers including BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, and UHC require prior authorization based on documented antidepressant failures and a DSM-5 MDD diagnosis. Session records are reviewed. Patients who miss a substantial portion of the course may have claims denied. If you are verifying coverage through a Bank of America or Truist employee benefits plan, ask your HR office specifically whether your plan follows standard BCBS NC or Cigna TMS medical policy criteria.

Side Effects That Can Affect Your Session Experience

TMS does not carry the systemic side effects associated with antidepressants — no weight changes, sexual side effects, or sedation. That said, TMS is not without side effects. The most commonly reported are scalp discomfort at the coil site and headache, particularly during the first one to two weeks. These typically diminish as treatment progresses and generally respond to over-the-counter analgesics.

Patients are fully awake and alert during every session — no anesthesia, no sedation required. You can drive yourself to and from appointments and return to work immediately afterward. This is a clinically meaningful distinction from electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), which requires anesthesia and a post-procedure recovery period. For more detail on the sensory experience, see our related article on what a TMS session feels like.

TMS Session Duration: Quick Reference

  • Standard rTMS (10 Hz): ~19–37 minutes per session
  • iTBS protocol: ~3 minutes per session (equivalent outcomes per Blumberger et al. 2018)
  • Accelerated / SAINT protocol: 10 sessions per day × 5 days (Cole et al. 2020)
  • Full course: 36 sessions over 6–8 weeks (standard for FDA-cleared depression treatment)
  • First session: Add ~10 minutes for motor threshold mapping
  • Post-session: No recovery time — patients drive themselves home

Bottom Line

A standard TMS session runs 19–37 minutes; iTBS protocols can be as short as 3 minutes. Either way, the full course is 36 sessions over 6–8 weeks — a meaningful time commitment that most working Charlotte adults manage with planning. Roughly 58% of treatment-resistant patients show a clinically meaningful response; approximately 42% do not.
