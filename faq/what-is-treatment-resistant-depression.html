What is treatment-resistant depression?

Quick Answer: Treatment-resistant depression is major depressive disorder that has not responded adequately to at least two antidepressants prescribed at therapeutic doses for adequate durations.

Key Takeaways

  • Treatment-resistant depression (TRD) is formally defined as failure to achieve adequate symptom relief after at least two antidepressant trials of adequate dose and duration — typically 4–6 weeks each.
  • Approximately 30% of people with major depressive disorder will meet criteria for treatment-resistant depression, according to NIMH-funded research including the STAR*D trial.
  • TMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation) is FDA-cleared specifically for treatment-resistant depression and produces a meaningful response in approximately 58% of TRD patients, with around 37% achieving full remission.
  • Most major insurers serving Charlotte — BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, and UnitedHealthcare — cover TMS for patients who meet TRD criteria: a DSM-5 MDD diagnosis plus documented failure of 2+ antidepressants.
  • If you are in crisis or experiencing suicidal thoughts, contact the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline immediately by calling or texting 988.

The Clinical Definition of Treatment-Resistant Depression

Treatment-resistant depression (TRD) is not a separate diagnosis — it is a clinical characterization of major depressive disorder (MDD) that has not responded to adequate treatment. The most widely used definition, consistent with NIMH research on treatment-resistant depression and the landmark STAR*D trial, requires that a patient fail to achieve adequate symptom relief after at least two antidepressant trials. Each trial must be conducted at a therapeutic dose for an adequate duration — typically a minimum of 4–6 weeks.

The word "resistant" can feel like a verdict. It is not. It is a clinical data point — one that tells your treatment team the brain's depression circuitry is not responding to pharmacological approaches alone, and that other interventions should be considered.

How Common Is Treatment-Resistant Depression?

The STAR*D trial, one of the largest real-world antidepressant studies ever conducted, followed over 4,000 patients with MDD. After four successive antidepressant trials, approximately 30% of patients still had not achieved sustained remission. A separate analysis published by researchers examining TRD epidemiology estimates that between 10–30% of all MDD patients will meet TRD criteria at some point in their illness.

Across Mecklenburg County's population of roughly 1.1 million, tens of thousands of adults are likely living with depression that antidepressants have not adequately controlled — including people who remain functional at work and are largely invisible as struggling.

What Actually Happens in the Brain With TRD?

Neuroimaging studies in MDD consistently show reduced activity in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) — a region involved in mood regulation, executive function, and emotional processing. Antidepressants work systemically, modulating neurotransmitter availability across the entire brain and body. When two or more of those medications fail, it suggests the DLPFC circuitry itself may be underresponsive to pharmacological stimulation.

TMS addresses this directly. A magnetic coil positioned over the left DLPFC delivers targeted magnetic pulses that depolarize cortical neurons, stimulating the circuit that antidepressants failed to adequately activate. This is a mechanistically distinct approach — not simply another pharmacological option.

💡 Clinical note: TMS has been FDA-cleared for treatment-resistant depression since 2008. It is not experimental. The O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite randomized controlled trial, published in Biological Psychiatry, was the pivotal evidence that led to that clearance — demonstrating statistically significant antidepressant effects versus sham TMS.

How Is TRD Diagnosed, and What Does It Mean for Insurance Coverage?

There is no blood test or imaging study that confirms TRD. The diagnosis is made clinically, based on documented treatment history. Your psychiatrist will review which antidepressants you have taken, at what doses, and for how long. If two or more trials meet the threshold for adequate dose and duration without sufficient relief, you meet the standard clinical and insurance criteria for TRD.

For TMS coverage specifically, most major insurers covering Charlotte-area patients apply similar criteria:

  • A current DSM-5 diagnosis of major depressive disorder
  • Documented failure of at least two antidepressants at adequate dose and duration
  • Prior authorization from the insurer before treatment begins

BCBS of North Carolina, Cigna, Aetna, and UnitedHealthcare all have active TMS coverage policies for qualifying TRD patients. Medicare covers TMS for MDD as well. Employees of self-insured large employers are typically covered under similar medical necessity criteria, though plan specifics vary. The licensed Charlotte TMS providers we connect you with can verify your specific coverage before your first session.

TRD is associated with significant mortality risk. Getting an accurate assessment is urgent — not optional.

What Are the Treatment Options Once TRD Is Confirmed?

The most evidence-supported next steps after antidepressant failure include:

  1. Augmentation strategies — adding lithium, atypical antipsychotics (aripiprazole, quetiapine), or thyroid hormone to an existing antidepressant
  2. Switching antidepressant class — moving from an SSRI to an SNRI, MAOI, or tricyclic
  3. TMS therapy — FDA-cleared, outpatient, no anesthesia, ~36 sessions over 6–7 weeks; approximately 58% of TRD patients show meaningful response in clinical trials (O'Reardon et al. 2007; George et al. 2010). The most common side effects are mild headache and scalp discomfort, which typically diminish after the first week.
  4. Esketamine (Spravato) — FDA-approved as a nasal spray for TRD; rapid-acting but requires in-office monitoring due to dissociative effects. Oral ketamine remains off-label for depression and is typically cash-pay.
  5. ECT (electroconvulsive therapy) — highly effective for severe TRD but requires anesthesia and carries risk of short-term memory side effects

TMS occupies a meaningful middle ground: more targeted than adding another medication, less invasive than ECT, FDA-cleared for TRD, and covered by insurance in a way ketamine infusions generally are not. For most patients, TMS is used alongside existing psychiatric care — it does not replace medication management or therapy.

Honest Numbers on TMS Response in TRD

In the O'Reardon et al. 2007 pivotal trial and subsequent real-world data, approximately 58% of TRD patients demonstrate a meaningful clinical response to a standard course of TMS. Around 37% achieve full remission. Roughly 42% do not respond adequately to TMS alone — a figure that belongs in every informed consent conversation. For more detail on what the research shows, see our article on what is the success rate of TMS for depression.

For patients who do not respond to standard TMS, accelerated protocols — including the Stanford SAINT protocol studied by Cole et al. 2020 — and deep TMS systems offer additional options. These remain evolving areas of research rather than established standard-of-care for all patients, but they are relevant for those who have not responded to first-line TMS.

Key Facts: Treatment-Resistant Depression at a Glance

  • Defined as failure of 2+ antidepressants at adequate dose and duration
  • Affects approximately 30% of all MDD patients (STAR*D trial data)
  • Diagnosed clinically — based on documented medication history
  • TMS is FDA-cleared specifically for TRD (2008); ~58% response rate in clinical trials, ~42% non-response rate
  • Common side effects include mild headache and scalp discomfort, particularly in early sessions
  • BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UHC, and Medicare cover TMS after documented TRD criteria are met
  • TMS works through direct cortical stimulation of the left DLPFC — a different mechanism than antidepressants
  • TMS is used alongside medication for most patients — it is not a replacement for psychiatric care
Bottom Line

Treatment-resistant depression is a well-defined clinical category — not a permanent prognosis. If you have tried two or more antidepressants without adequate relief, you likely meet the criteria that insurers use to authorize TMS. Approximately 58% of TRD patients respond to a standard TMS course; approximately 42% do not, and that figure should be part of any informed consent conversation.
