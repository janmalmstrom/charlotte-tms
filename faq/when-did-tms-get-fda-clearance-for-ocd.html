When did TMS get FDA clearance for OCD?

Quick Answer: The FDA cleared TMS for OCD in 2018, specifically the Brainsway H7 deep TMS coil, making it the first TMS device cleared for obsessive-compulsive disorder.

Key Takeaways

  • The FDA issued 510(k) clearance for deep TMS (Brainsway H7 coil) for OCD in 2018 — a separate clearance from the 2008 depression clearance.
  • The pivotal clinical trial by Carmi et al. showed a 38% response rate in OCD patients who had not responded adequately to medication and therapy.
  • OCD clearance requires the Brainsway H7 coil specifically — standard figure-8 coils used for depression are not FDA-cleared for OCD.
  • Most major insurers, including BCBS NC and Cigna, are expanding OCD coverage, but criteria vary significantly from depression TMS coverage; prior authorization is typically required.
  • TMS for OCD targets the medial prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex — a different brain circuit than the left DLPFC targeted in depression treatment.

The 2018 FDA Clearance: What It Covers and What It Doesn't

The FDA issued 510(k) clearance for transcranial magnetic stimulation as an adjunct treatment for obsessive-compulsive disorder in August 2018. That clearance was granted specifically to Brainsway's H7 deep TMS coil system — not to TMS devices broadly. If you're evaluating a clinic for OCD treatment, the first question to ask is whether they operate a Brainsway H7 system. A standard figure-8 coil used for depression is not FDA-cleared for OCD, and the distinction is clinically meaningful: OCD requires stimulation of deeper cortical structures than depression does.

To be precise about the regulatory language: TMS is FDA-cleared via the 510(k) pathway, not FDA-approved through the NDA process. The clearance covers TMS as an adjunct treatment — meaning it is used alongside existing medication and behavioral therapy, not as a standalone replacement for either.

The Clinical Trial Behind the Clearance: Carmi et al.

The FDA's 2018 OCD clearance was grounded in a pivotal multicenter trial led by Carmi et al., published in the American Journal of Psychiatry. The trial enrolled 99 patients with OCD who had an inadequate response to first-line treatment — typically a combination of an SSRI and exposure and response prevention (ERP) therapy. Patients received 29 deep TMS sessions over six weeks. The primary outcome measure was the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS).

Results: 38% of patients in the active stimulation group met response criteria (a ≥30% reduction in Y-BOCS score), compared to 11% in the sham group. That is a clinically meaningful signal in a population that had already failed standard treatment. You can review the Carmi et al. trial data on PubMed for the full methodology and outcomes.

Roughly 62% of patients in the trial did not meet the response threshold. TMS for OCD is a legitimate clinical option with meaningful evidence — it is not a solution for every patient.

💡 Clinical note: The OCD brain circuit targeted by deep TMS — the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) — sits anatomically deeper than the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) targeted in depression treatment. The H7 coil reaches 3–4 cm below the scalp; standard figure-8 coils reach approximately 2–3 cm. That difference in penetration depth is why device type matters for OCD eligibility.

How OCD TMS Differs from Depression TMS in Practice

Beyond the coil difference, the OCD protocol includes a feature absent from standard depression TMS: brief symptom provocation before each session. Patients are exposed to an OCD-relevant stimulus — an image, object, or scenario — that activates the OCD circuit immediately before stimulation begins. This provocation-then-stimulate sequence is built into the Brainsway OCD protocol and is part of what the FDA clearance covers.

Session counts also differ. The Carmi trial used 29 sessions over six weeks; clinical practice may range from 29 to 36 sessions depending on provider protocol and patient response. Sessions typically run 20 minutes. The American Psychiatric Association's OCD treatment guidelines position TMS as an adjunct option for patients with inadequate response to first-line therapies.

Insurance Coverage for OCD TMS in North Carolina

Coverage for OCD TMS in North Carolina is more variable than coverage for depression TMS. Depression TMS has been FDA-cleared since 2008 — insurers have had over 15 years to develop coverage policies. OCD clearance arrived in 2018, and carrier policies are still catching up.

BCBS of North Carolina covers TMS for depression and has been expanding OCD coverage, but typically requires documented failure of at least one SSRI at an adequate dose and duration, plus documented failure of ERP therapy. Cigna and Aetna apply similar criteria. UnitedHealthcare policies vary by employer plan. If you are covered through a large Charlotte employer — Bank of America, Truist, Atrium Health, Novant Health, or similar — your plan's OCD TMS criteria may differ from the general insurer policy. Self-funded group plans sometimes operate under different coverage rules than fully-insured plans.

⚠️ Important: Do not assume OCD TMS coverage based on your insurer covering TMS for depression. OCD and depression carry separate coverage policies with separate clinical criteria. Verification before starting treatment is essential. Licensed Charlotte TMS providers can help navigate the prior authorization process with your specific insurer.

OCD TMS: Key Clinical Facts at a Glance

  • FDA clearance issued: August 2018
  • Device cleared: Brainsway H7 deep TMS coil (not standard figure-8 coils)
  • Indication: adjunct treatment for OCD in adults
  • Pivotal trial: Carmi et al. — 38% response rate vs. 11% sham (Y-BOCS ≥30% reduction)
  • Target brain circuit: medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)
  • Typical protocol: 29–36 sessions, includes symptom provocation before each session
  • Used alongside, not instead of, SSRI medication and ERP therapy for most patients

Bottom Line

The FDA cleared TMS for OCD in 2018 — specifically the Brainsway H7 deep TMS coil — based on the Carmi et al. trial showing a 38% response rate in patients with inadequate response to medication and ERP therapy. Roughly 62% of patients in that trial did not meet the response threshold. OCD TMS is a legitimate, FDA-cleared option for treatment-resistant OCD; it is not a guaranteed solution. Insurance coverage in North Carolina is expanding but requires separate verification from depression TMS coverage.
