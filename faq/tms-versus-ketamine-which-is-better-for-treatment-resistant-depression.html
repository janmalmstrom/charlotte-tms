TMS versus ketamine which is better for treatment-resistant depression?

Quick Answer: TMS is FDA-cleared and covered by most major insurers after antidepressant failure; ketamine works faster but is off-label for depression, cash-pay only, and carries dissociative side effects — making TMS the better-supported first choice for most treatment-resistant patients.

Key Takeaways

  • TMS has been FDA-cleared for treatment-resistant depression since 2008 and produces a ~58% response rate in patients who have failed 2+ antidepressants, per the O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite trial.
  • Ketamine (IV infusion) is FDA-approved only for anesthesia; its use for depression is off-label. Esketamine nasal spray (Spravato) has FDA approval for treatment-resistant depression but still requires in-office administration and is not covered by all plans.
  • BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UnitedHealthcare, and Medicare typically cover TMS after documented failure of 2+ adequate antidepressant trials — ketamine infusions are almost always cash-pay, ranging from $400–$800 per infusion.
  • TMS requires 36 outpatient sessions over 6–8 weeks; patients drive themselves to and from every session with no sedation, no anesthesia, and no dissociative effect.
  • Roughly 42% of TMS patients do not achieve a meaningful response — honest patient selection and realistic expectations matter before committing to either treatment.

The Core Clinical Difference: Regulatory Status and How Each Treatment Works

If you've tried two or more antidepressants without adequate relief, you're evaluating real options — not theoretical ones. TMS and ketamine are the two non-antidepressant treatments most patients in Charlotte ask about, so the comparison deserves a straightforward answer.

TMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation) delivers focused magnetic pulses to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) — a region consistently underactive in major depressive disorder. Repeated stimulation over 36 sessions drives neuroplastic changes that restore more normal circuit activity. It has been FDA-cleared for treatment-resistant depression since 2008, following the O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite randomized controlled trial published in Biological Psychiatry, which demonstrated statistically significant improvement over sham treatment with no serious adverse events.

Ketamine works through a different mechanism entirely: it blocks NMDA glutamate receptors, producing rapid — sometimes within hours — antidepressant effects. The IV form used in infusion clinics is FDA-approved only as an anesthetic. Its use for depression is off-label. Esketamine (Spravato), the nasal spray derived from ketamine, received FDA approval in 2019 specifically for treatment-resistant depression, but it must be administered in a certified healthcare setting and carries a REMS (Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy) requirement because of dissociation and abuse potential.

Speed vs. Durability: What the Evidence Actually Shows

Ketamine's most cited advantage is speed. A single IV infusion can produce measurable antidepressant effects within 24–72 hours. For patients in acute crisis, that speed matters. The limitation is durability: most studies show the antidepressant effect of a single ketamine infusion fades within 1–2 weeks. Repeated infusions extend that window, but long-term maintenance data are limited and the optimal dosing schedule is not yet standardized.

TMS works more gradually. Most patients notice mood changes around weeks 3–4 of the standard 36-session protocol. The payoff is durability: a meaningful proportion of TMS responders maintain improvement for 12 months or longer without retreatment, according to durability data from George et al. 2010 in the Archives of General Psychiatry. For a professional working in Charlotte's banking or finance sector — at Bank of America, Truist, or one of the firms along the I-77 corridor — the ability to complete a 20-minute outpatient TMS session before work, drive yourself home, and maintain full cognitive function throughout is a practical advantage that IV ketamine's dissociative effects do not allow.

💡 Clinical note: Ketamine's dissociative effect means you cannot drive for the remainder of the day after an infusion. TMS produces no sedation, no cognitive impairment, and no restrictions on driving or working — patients at our Charlotte TMS clinic routinely schedule sessions before or after their workday.

Insurance Coverage: The Practical Reality in North Carolina

For most patients, insurance coverage is the deciding factor — and here the difference is stark.

  • TMS: BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UnitedHealthcare, and Medicare cover TMS for treatment-resistant depression after documented failure of at least two adequate antidepressant trials at therapeutic doses, with a DSM-5 MDD diagnosis. Medicaid coverage in North Carolina varies by plan — verify your specific policy.
  • IV ketamine infusions: Almost universally not covered by commercial insurance. A typical course of six infusions runs $2,400–$4,800 out of pocket in the Charlotte metro area.
  • Esketamine (Spravato): Coverage is more variable. Some commercial plans cover it after prior authorization; others do not. It requires in-office administration every 1–2 weeks indefinitely for many patients, creating an ongoing cost burden even when partially covered.

If you want to verify your insurance coverage before making any decision, our team can check your BCBS, Cigna, Aetna, or UHC benefits and confirm eligibility criteria before your first appointment.

Side Effects: An Honest Comparison

Neither treatment is free of side effects, and you should know what you're signing up for.

TMS Side Effects

  • Scalp discomfort or mild headache during and shortly after sessions — typically resolves within the first week as the scalp adjusts
  • Rare risk of seizure (~0.1%), most often associated with protocol deviations
  • No systemic effects: no weight changes, no sexual side effects, no cognitive impairment

Ketamine / Esketamine Side Effects

  • Dissociation, perceptual disturbances, dizziness — during and immediately after infusion
  • Nausea, elevated blood pressure
  • Potential for dependence and misuse with repeated use — the reason FDA requires REMS for Spravato
  • Long-term neurological effects of repeated ketamine use in humans are not fully characterized
⚠️ Important: Ketamine and esketamine carry a risk of dissociation, elevated blood pressure, and — with repeated use — potential for abuse. The FDA's REMS program for Spravato requires that every dose be administered in a certified medical setting with a 2-hour post-dose monitoring period. This is not a treatment you take at home.

Which Should You Choose? A Clinical Framework

The answer depends on your clinical situation, insurance coverage, and what you need from treatment. According to NIMH brain stimulation research, TMS and ketamine occupy different positions in the treatment algorithm — they are not strict alternatives in every case.

TMS Is Likely the Better First Choice If:

  • You have failed 2+ antidepressants and have a DSM-5 MDD diagnosis (meeting standard insurance criteria)
  • You are insured through BCBS NC, Cigna, Aetna, UHC, or Medicare and want coverage
  • You cannot tolerate dissociation or sedation during treatment
  • You need to drive, work, or maintain full cognitive function throughout your treatment course
  • You want durable remission with established long-term follow-up data

Ketamine May Be Worth Discussing With Your Psychiatrist If:

  • You are in acute suicidal crisis and need rapid — within hours — symptom reduction
  • You can pay out of pocket and are willing to accept shorter-duration effects
  • You have already completed a full TMS course without adequate response
  • Your treating psychiatrist at Atrium Health or Novant Health has recommended it as a bridge treatment

Importantly, these treatments are not always mutually exclusive. Some patients complete TMS and, if they experience a partial response, discuss ketamine as an augmentation strategy with their psychiatrist. That decision requires physician oversight — not a wellness clinic.

Bottom Line

For most treatment-resistant depression patients in Charlotte and Mecklenburg County, TMS is the stronger first-line choice between these two options: it is FDA-cleared, covered by major insurers after antidepressant failure, produces durable response in ~58% of patients, and carries no dissociative or sedative effects. Ketamine works faster but lasts shorter, is almost always cash-pay, and carries an abuse-potential profile that requires ongoing clinical monitoring. Neither treatment works for everyone — roughly 42% of TMS patients do not achieve a meaningful response, and ketamine's durability data remain limited. The right answer for you depends on your insurance, your clinical history, and your psychiatrist's assessment.
