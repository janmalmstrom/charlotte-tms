What Is TMS and Who Qualifies?

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a non-invasive brain stimulation therapy FDA-cleared for major depressive disorder since 2008, for OCD since 2018 (Brainsway H7 coil only), for anxious depression since 2021 (NeuroStar), and for smoking cessation since 2020. A magnetic coil positioned against the scalp delivers focused pulses to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) — a region consistently underactive in patients with major depressive disorder, as documented in NIMH brain stimulation research.

The pivotal multisite trial by O'Reardon et al. (2007) established clinical efficacy in treatment-resistant patients; George et al. (2010) confirmed those findings in a large NIMH-funded randomized controlled trial. In treatment-resistant depression, the response rate is approximately 58% and remission occurs in roughly 37% of patients. To state the inverse plainly: approximately 42% of treatment-resistant patients do not achieve a meaningful response. TMS is a clinically supported option — not a certainty.

Standard qualifying criteria:

  • DSM-5 diagnosis of major depressive disorder (MDD)
  • Documented failure of two or more adequate antidepressant trials
  • No metal implants in or near the head
  • No active seizure disorder
💡 Clinical note: "Failure" in insurance language means an adequate trial — typically a therapeutic dose for at least 4–6 weeks — not simply a trial discontinued after a week due to side effects. The provider's intake team will review your medication history to confirm you meet the documented-failure threshold before submitting for insurance authorization.

What Does a TMS Course Actually Look Like for a Concord Patient?

A standard TMS course consists of 36 sessions, typically five days per week for six weeks, with a brief taper period. Each session lasts approximately 20–40 minutes depending on the protocol — standard repetitive TMS (rTMS) or intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS). During the session, you sit in a reclined chair, fully awake, while the magnetic coil delivers pulses to the scalp. No anesthesia. No sedation. Patients drive themselves to and from every appointment.

Side effects should be disclosed clearly. The most commonly reported are mild-to-moderate headache and scalp discomfort at the treatment site, documented in approximately 30% of patients across clinical trials. These typically diminish after the first week of treatment. Serious adverse events, including seizure, are rare — estimated at less than 1 in 10,000 sessions in properly screened patients.

How Do You Get Started?

The process begins with a psychiatric consultation — either with the licensed Charlotte TMS provider we connect you with, or in coordination with your existing psychiatrist at a practice such as Atrium Health's behavioral health network or Novant Health Psychiatric Associates. Some patients reduce medication doses after a successful TMS course; that decision is made by your treating physician based on your individual clinical response.
