Is TMS therapy safe?

Quick Answer: TMS therapy is FDA-cleared and has a well-established safety profile backed by 15+ years of clinical use, though common side effects include headaches and scalp discomfort.

Key Takeaways

  • TMS received its first FDA 510(k) clearance for major depressive disorder in 2008; it has since been cleared for OCD (2018), anxious depression (2021), and smoking cessation (2020).
  • The most commonly reported side effects are headaches and scalp discomfort at the stimulation site, occurring in a meaningful minority of patients — not a negligible number. These typically diminish after the first week of treatment.
  • Seizure is the most serious reported risk, occurring in roughly 1 in 30,000 sessions in the published literature — a rate comparable to seizure risk associated with antidepressant medications.
  • The O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite trial (published in Biological Psychiatry) was the pivotal FDA registration study; it found TMS significantly superior to sham for treatment-resistant depression with an acceptable adverse event profile.
  • TMS is performed while patients are awake, requires no anesthesia, and patients drive themselves to and from every one of the standard 36 sessions — a meaningful practical safety advantage over procedures requiring sedation.

What the FDA Clearance Record Actually Tells You

TMS is FDA-cleared — specifically through the 510(k) clearance pathway, which evaluates safety and substantial equivalence to a legally marketed predicate device. This is not the same as FDA drug approval (the NDA process), but it is a rigorous regulatory standard. The first TMS system for major depressive disorder, NeuroStar, received 510(k) clearance in 2008. Since then, the FDA has cleared additional systems and indications: the Brainsway H7 for OCD in 2018, NeuroStar for anxious depression in 2021, and a system for smoking cessation in 2020. Device clearance records are publicly searchable through the FDA's device clearance database.

That record matters because it reflects safety data submitted to a federal regulatory agency — not manufacturer claims alone. It now spans more than 15 years of post-market surveillance alongside the original trial data.

What the Clinical Trials Show About Safety

The pivotal registration study — O'Reardon et al. 2007, a multisite randomized controlled trial published in Biological Psychiatry — found TMS superior to sham for treatment-resistant depression with an acceptable adverse event profile. Headache was the most commonly reported side effect. No serious neurological adverse events were attributed to active treatment.

Safety data indexed on PubMed consistently identifies two adverse event categories:

  • Common and manageable: Headache (reported in approximately 30% of patients across trials) and scalp discomfort at the coil site. Both typically decrease in intensity after the first one to two weeks of the standard 36-session protocol.
  • Rare and serious: Seizure. Published data places the risk at approximately 1 in 30,000 sessions — comparable to the seizure risk associated with bupropion and tricyclic antidepressants. Proper pre-treatment screening eliminates most of this risk.
⚠️ Important: TMS is contraindicated for patients with ferromagnetic metal implants in or near the skull — including certain cochlear implants, aneurysm clips, and deep brain stimulators. A thorough medical history and screening questionnaire are completed before any course of treatment begins. Do not begin TMS without disclosing your full implant history to the treating provider.

What TMS Does Not Involve — No Anesthesia, No Sedation

Nothing is injected, ingested, or implanted. You sit in a reclined chair, a magnetic coil is positioned against your scalp, and a series of magnetic pulses are delivered to a targeted brain region — typically the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), which shows consistently reduced activity in patients with major depressive disorder. Each session takes approximately 20–40 minutes. You remain awake throughout and drive yourself home afterward.

This is a meaningful procedural distinction from electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), which requires general anesthesia, carries a risk of post-procedure memory disruption, and typically mandates a recovery period. TMS carries none of those procedural risks.

💡 Clinical note: Most patients at licensed Charlotte TMS providers continue existing psychiatric medications during a TMS course. TMS works through direct neural stimulation rather than systemic pharmacology and is used alongside medication for many patients — not as a replacement. Do not adjust your medication without guidance from your prescribing psychiatrist.

Long-Term Safety: Post-Market Surveillance Since 2008

Post-market surveillance has not identified new serious safety signals beyond those established in the original trials. No evidence of cumulative neurological harm from repeated TMS courses at standard clinical parameters has emerged. Patients who return for maintenance TMS — typically a smaller number of booster sessions following an initial 36-session course — are not exposed to escalating risk.

The Blumberger et al. 2018 trial published in The Lancet compared intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) to standard repetitive TMS and found comparable safety profiles between protocols. iTBS is now used in both accelerated and standard treatment formats and is cleared for clinical use.

TMS Safety: Key Clinical Facts

  • FDA-cleared via 510(k) pathway since 2008 for major depressive disorder
  • Most common side effects: headache (~30% of patients) and scalp discomfort — both manageable and typically diminish within the first two weeks
  • Seizure risk: approximately 1 in 30,000 sessions; comparable to antidepressant-associated seizure risk
  • No anesthesia, no sedation — patients drive themselves to and from every session
  • Contraindicated in patients with ferromagnetic metal implants in or near the skull
  • 15+ years of post-market surveillance with no new serious safety signals identified
  • Most patients continue psychiatric medications during TMS; it is not a medication replacement
Bottom Line

TMS therapy has a well-established safety profile supported by the O'Reardon et al. 2007 registration trial, 15+ years of FDA-cleared clinical use, and consistent post-market surveillance data. The most common adverse effects — headache and scalp discomfort — are real, affect a meaningful minority of patients, and typically diminish over the first two weeks of treatment. Serious risks, particularly seizure, are rare (approximately 1 in 30,000 sessions) and are further reduced by proper pre-treatment screening. TMS does not produce a response in all patients — approximately 42% of treatment-resistant patients do not show meaningful improvement — but the risk profile is well-characterized and compares favorably to many pharmacological alternatives.
