What are the side effects of TMS therapy?

Quick Answer: The most common TMS side effects are headache and scalp discomfort at the treatment site, occurring in roughly 30–40% of patients; serious adverse events, including seizure, are rare.

Key Takeaways

  • Headache and scalp discomfort are the most frequently reported TMS side effects, documented in approximately 30–40% of patients across clinical trials — both are typically mild and resolve within the first two weeks of treatment.
  • Seizure is the most serious known risk; the estimated incidence is approximately 1 in 10,000 treatment courses, lower than the seizure risk associated with several antidepressants.
  • Unlike ECT, TMS does not require anesthesia, produces no cognitive impairment, and does not cause memory loss — patients drive themselves to and from every session.
  • TMS does not cause the systemic side effects associated with antidepressant medication — no weight gain, no sexual dysfunction, no GI upset — because it acts locally on the brain rather than through the bloodstream.
  • The O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite trial (the pivotal FDA clearance study) found no significant difference in seizure rates between active TMS and sham, and the most common adverse event was headache, which decreased in frequency over the treatment course.

What Side Effects Does TMS Actually Cause?

If you've tried two or more antidepressants and are weighing TMS as your next option, you deserve a direct answer — not a list of reassurances. So here it is: TMS does have side effects. They are mostly local, mostly mild, and mostly temporary. Understanding what they are — and why they occur — helps you make an informed decision.

TMS (Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation) has been FDA-cleared for treatment-resistant depression since 2008. The safety profile that follows reflects data from the pivotal O'Reardon et al. 2007 multisite trial, subsequent large-scale studies, and FDA device clearance documentation — not marketing claims. You can review the FDA's TMS device clearance records directly at the FDA's device clearance database.

The Most Common Side Effects: Headache and Scalp Discomfort

In clinical trials, headache and scalp discomfort at the coil placement site are the most consistently reported side effects, affecting roughly 30–40% of patients at some point during a treatment course. These effects occur because the magnetic pulses stimulate both the target brain tissue (the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex) and the scalp muscles and nerves directly beneath the coil.

A few important clinical details about these effects:

  • Timing: They are most likely during the first week of treatment. Most patients notice significant reduction in headache and scalp sensitivity by weeks two to three.
  • Severity: The majority of patients describe these as mild to moderate. Severe headache is uncommon.
  • Management: Over-the-counter analgesics (ibuprofen, acetaminophen) taken about an hour before a session can reduce headache. Your treating psychiatrist can also adjust coil positioning or stimulation parameters if discomfort is significant.
  • Duration: Headache typically resolves within a few hours of each session, not between sessions.
💡 Clinical note: If you work in a demanding professional environment — at Bank of America, Truist, or Atrium Health, for example — you should know that most patients return to work immediately after their TMS session. The typical 37-minute session produces no sedation, no cognitive impairment, and no restriction on driving.

Less Common Side Effects Worth Knowing

Beyond headache and scalp discomfort, a smaller proportion of patients report the following:

  • Facial twitching or jaw clenching during the session: The magnetic pulse activates nearby motor neurons, which can cause brief, involuntary muscle contractions. This stops the moment the pulse train ends. It is not harmful.
  • Lightheadedness immediately after a session: Typically brief — resolves within minutes.
  • Temporary hearing discomfort: The TMS coil produces a loud clicking sound (~70–100 dB). Earplugs or foam inserts are provided at every session. Audiometric testing in clinical trials has not shown persistent hearing changes when ear protection is used.
  • Neck or jaw stiffness: Reported by a smaller subset of patients, related to muscle activation during treatment. Usually resolves within the first week.

The Serious Risk: Seizure

Seizure is the most serious known adverse event associated with TMS. It is also rare. The estimated incidence across clinical use is approximately 1 in 10,000 treatment courses. To put that in clinical context: several commonly prescribed antidepressants — including bupropion at higher doses — carry a comparable or higher seizure risk.

Clinics performing TMS follow established safety screening protocols specifically designed to identify patients at elevated seizure risk before starting treatment. These include reviewing your medication list (certain drugs lower seizure threshold), your neurological history, and any history of head injury. A thorough intake assessment at our Charlotte TMS clinic is the first line of seizure risk reduction.

⚠️ Important: If you have a history of epilepsy, a prior seizure, or a known seizure disorder, TMS may not be appropriate for you. This is determined during your pre-treatment psychiatric evaluation — not at your first session. Be thorough and transparent during your intake consultation.

What TMS Does Not Cause

Patients who have spent years managing antidepressant side effects — weight gain, sexual dysfunction, GI disturbance, emotional blunting — often ask whether TMS carries similar systemic risks. The short answer is no, and here is why: TMS acts locally. The magnetic field depolarizes neurons in a targeted cortical region. It does not circulate through your bloodstream, interact with your liver enzymes, or affect your endocrine system.

Specific side effects associated with antidepressants that TMS does not cause:

  • Weight gain or metabolic changes
  • Sexual dysfunction (common with SSRIs and SNRIs)
  • GI upset, nausea, or diarrhea
  • Sedation or cognitive dulling
  • Memory impairment (a known risk of ECT — not TMS)
  • Withdrawal syndrome upon stopping treatment

It is worth stating clearly: most patients continue their current psychiatric medication during and after a TMS course. TMS is a non-pharmacological intervention used alongside medication for many patients — it is not a blanket replacement for your prescriptions, and you should not adjust your medication without direct guidance from your psychiatrist.

TMS Side Effects at a Glance

  • Common (30–40%): Headache, scalp discomfort — usually mild, improves over weeks 1–2
  • Less common: Facial twitching during pulse delivery, lightheadedness, temporary jaw tension
  • Rare (~1 in 10,000): Seizure — mitigated by pre-treatment safety screening
  • Not reported: Weight gain, sexual dysfunction, sedation, memory loss, withdrawal
  • Post-session function: Patients drive themselves home and return to work immediately

Bottom Line

TMS therapy is not side-effect-free, but its side effect profile is well-characterized and — for most patients — substantially more tolerable than another round of antidepressants. Headache and scalp discomfort affect roughly 30–40% of patients and typically diminish over the first two weeks. Seizure risk is real but rare (~1 in 10,000 treatment courses) and is screened for before treatment begins. TMS produces no systemic side effects and no cognitive impairment. Approximately 58% of treatment-resistant patients show a meaningful clinical response; ~42% do not. Side effect tolerability should be weighed against the documented burden of untreated or inadequately treated depression — that is a conversation to have with your psychiatrist, not a website.
